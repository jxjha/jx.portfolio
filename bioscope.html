01

A rupee to look

When I was a kid in India, a man would arrive on a bicycle with a painted wooden box on the back. You paid him a rupee, he turned a crank, and you put your eye to a brass hole and watched pictures crawl past while he sang over them.

The box was called a bioscope. It was vermilion and mustard, covered in hand-painted marigolds, with a gramophone horn bolted to the lid and a signboard across the front that almost always read देखो मगर प्यार से, look, but look with love.

There was nothing inside it but a strip of paper and a light bulb. The pictures weren't special. The way you were made to look at them was. You had to bend down. You had to wait your turn. You had to give the man a rupee. By the time your eye reached the glass you were already paying attention.

They're nearly gone now. A phone shows you more pictures in a second than a bioscope-wala showed in a career, and none of them ask anything of you.
02

Why it stuck

The bioscope taught me something I've never stopped believing: how you show something is part of what you're showing.

The same photographs, printed in a book, would have been forgettable. Put them behind brass and a crank and they become an event. The container did the work. Not decoration on top of the content: the container was part of the content.

That idea is why I keep refusing to present work as a list of bullet points. A dashboard nobody opens is a failed dashboard, however correct the SQL. Training videos nobody watches are a failed pipeline, however clever the AI. The delivery is not the wrapper around the work. It's half the work.

03

Every page here is a bioscope

I built this whole portfolio before I noticed I'd been building bioscopes the entire time. Each one is an object you operate rather than a page you read, and each one makes you do a small piece of work before it gives you anything.

Walk it

A city of work

Every place I've worked, as a street you drive down. Buildings rise as you reach them.
Fly it

A room in space

Photographs floating along a path. You cruise past them instead of scrolling a grid.
Play it

A record library

Beats as a crate of vinyl. Sleeves you pull out, a needle you drop.
Fly it

A drone survey

A rooftop rebuilt in 3D, flown through stage by stage until it becomes an energy figure.

None of that is decoration. It's the same bet the bioscope-wala made: make someone participate, and they'll remember what they saw.

04

How this one is built

There isn't a single texture file, model file or audio file in this thing. The paint, the brass, the Devanagari signboard, the ratchet and the music are all generated the moment the page loads.

Painted at runtime

No texture files

The vermilion woodgrain, the marigolds, the pierced tin fretwork and the brushed brass are drawn onto 2D canvases in code, then wrapped onto the geometry. Seeded randomness means the paintwork looks identical every reload.

Synthesised

No audio files

The ratchet click is filtered white noise with a wooden thunk under it; the shutter chime is three sine partials. The music is four ragas, Bhairav, Yaman, Bhupali, Bageshri, played as a tanpura drone under a plucked line that wanders rather than loops.

Hand feel

The crank has physics

Hold it and it winds at a steady pace. Drag it and it follows your pointer around the axle. Let go mid-drag and it coasts down through friction. Twelve notches a turn, one new picture per full turn.

It was originally written for React and React-Three-Fiber. This site has no build step, it's a folder of files, so the whole thing was ported to plain three.js rather than dragging a framework in behind it. Keeping the site simple was worth more than the convenience of the original code.
