01

The question

Everyone knows roughly how sunny a region is. Almost nobody knows how much energy one specific roof could actually produce — because that depends on the roof's exact shape, tilt, orientation, and what shades it through the year.

Regional context — Sachsen-Anhalt sits around 1,000–1,100 kWh/kWp a year. A useful start, but a regional average can't tell you what a single building will do.

So the project moved from the map to the building: capture one real rooftop precisely enough to model its production, not estimate it. The tool for "precisely enough" is a drone.

02

The capture

A UAV flew a programmed grid over the building, shooting ~100 overlapping photographs of the roof and façades. Overlap is everything — every point on the roof has to appear in several frames from different angles for the reconstruction to triangulate it.

CAPTURE 01

Programmed grid

An automated flight path so coverage and overlap are consistent, not left to a pilot's judgement.

CAPTURE 02

~100 photos

High overlap across roof and façades — the raw material photogrammetry needs to rebuild geometry.

CAPTURE 03

Nadir + oblique

Straight-down and angled frames together, so vertical surfaces and roof edges reconstruct cleanly.

03

Photos → 3D model

Photogrammetry turns those overlapping frames into a dense point cloud, then a textured 3D surface — a true-to-life digital twin of the building you can measure, not just look at.

Textured mesh — the reconstructed surface with real photographic texture. Every roof plane, ledge and rooftop unit is now measurable geometry.
04

Height & elevation

The same model, re-coloured by elevation — a Digital Surface Model. Drag the divider: on one side the photo texture, on the other the height map that drives every downstream calculation.

Photo texture Elevation (DSM)

◆ drag to wipe between texture and elevation

Contours — height stepped into readable bands, the way a surveyor reads a roof.
Height distribution — the roof's elevation spread, isolating the flat, usable planes from everything else.
05

Roof planes & solar zones

From the model, the usable roof planes are isolated and measured — the cyan zones mark where panels would actually go, excluding ridges, edges and obstructions. Roughly 1,600 m² of candidate surface.

Panel zones — usable roof area marked in cyan, laid over the elevation map. This is the surface the yield estimate is built on.

In QGIS, solar radiation is modelled across those planes — factoring local irradiance, panel tilt, azimuth and seasonal shading — resolving to a Harz-region figure of roughly 1,050 kWh/m²/yr of usable input.

06

The yield

Usable area × panel density × modelled irradiance → a real production estimate. Not a regional guess — a number for this roof.

The point wasn't the single number — it was the method: a repeatable pipeline that turns a short drone flight into a defensible, per-building solar business case. Spatial data is one of the most underused business-intelligence tools there is, and this is what it looks like put to work.
